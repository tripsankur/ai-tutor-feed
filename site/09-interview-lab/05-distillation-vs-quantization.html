Distillation vs Quantization: Advantages & Disadvantages

19 min listen · The master painting, the apprentice's smaller canvas, and the reprint with fewer shades: how distillation and quantization each make a model cheaper, what each one costs and breaks, when to stack them, and how to answer the pros-and-cons question in ninety seconds.

Facts verified: 2026-09-25

Thesis: Distillation changes what the model is (a smaller student trained to imitate a teacher); quantization changes how precisely the same model is stored (fewer bits per number). One is a training project with a capability ceiling, the other is a compression step with non-uniform damage, and the strongest production move is often both: distill, then quantize the student.

Interview question (as asked): "Talk me through distillation and quantization — what are the pros and cons of each?"

Minimal logit-KD loss (PyTorch sketch):

import torch.nn.functional as F

def kd_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=2.0, alpha=0.5):
    # soft part: match teacher's softened distribution (forward KL)
    soft = F.kl_div(
        F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1),
        F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1),
        reduction="batchmean",
    ) * (T * T)
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits.view(-1, student_logits.size(-1)), labels.view(-1))
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard

Hugging Face TRL ships GKDTrainer (on-policy / mixed) for the on-policy flavour.

Quantization mechanics

Scheme What's low-precision Where it helps Notes
W4A16 (AWQ, GPTQ) Weights only (4-bit), math in 16-bit Memory-bound decode, small batches, fitting on smaller GPUs ~4× less weight memory; win fades when compute-bound

Memory arithmetic (weights only): 70B × 2 bytes ≈ 140 GB (BF16) → ≈ 70 GB (8-bit) → ≈ 35–40 GB (4-bit incl. scales).

Key evidence: - "Give Me BF16 or Give Me Death" (500k+ evals, Llama 3.1): FP8 W8A8 lossless; tuned INT8 W8A8 1–3% drop; INT4 W4A16 competitive with INT8. - "Does quantization affect models' performance on long-context tasks?" (EMNLP 2025): 8-bit ≈ 0.8% avg drop; 4-bit up to 59% drop on long-input tasks, worse with longer context and non-English.

Decision framework

  1. Fast win, supported hardware → FP8 first.
  2. Memory-bound, small batch, short context → W4A16 (AWQ/GPTQ) + slice-level eval.
  3. Narrow, high-volume, stable task, weeks available → distill → quantize student (consider QAT).
  4. Broad / fast-changing task → avoid heavy distillation; quantization + routing; keep teacher fallback.
  5. Always: same eval gate, paired comparison vs original, shadow traffic, rollback path.

War story (generic)

An internal ops assistant moved to 4-bit weight-only to fit a cheaper GPU; general benchmark within a point. A week later: tool-call JSON arguments drifting (date formats, transposed digits, truncated payloads) and long-document summaries looping past ~30k tokens. Fix: FP8 for that workload, 4-bit only for a short-context classifier, and add "tool-arg exactness" and "long-context faithfulness" slices to the release gate.

5 interview questions with strong answers

1. Pros and cons of distillation vs quantization? Distillation trains a smaller student to imitate a teacher — big savings in params, compute and KV cache, and task specialization; cons are a weeks-long training project, a capability ceiling, off-distribution degradation, and a new model to validate (plus licensing on teacher outputs). Quantization keeps the model and stores numbers in fewer bits — hours of work, reversible, FP8 near-lossless, 4-bit quarters weight memory; cons are non-uniform damage (long context, math, structured output) and hardware/kernel-dependent speedups.

2. Does quantization improve latency or throughput? Weight-only (W4A16) mainly speeds the memory-bound decode phase — better time-per-output-token at small batch. Weight+activation (W8A8 INT8/FP8) uses low-precision tensor cores, improving compute-bound large-batch throughput. KV-cache quantization is separate and raises concurrency/context capacity.

3. How do you validate a quantized model before production? Not perplexity alone. Run the task eval set with slices for long inputs, numbers, tool-call argument exactness, refusals; paired comparison against the BF16 model with confidence intervals; calibrate on data resembling production; shadow live traffic; record scheme + calibration set for audit.

4. Logit vs sequence-level vs on-policy distillation? Logit KD matches the teacher's full distribution (needs logits, shared tokenizer). Sequence-level KD fine-tunes on teacher outputs (works with hosted teachers, suffers exposure bias). On-policy KD has the student generate and the teacher grade each token with reverse KL — fixes exposure bias, costs an RL-like loop.

5. When would you combine them, and in which order? Distill first (architectural win), then quantize the student (another 2–4× memory). Small students are more fragile under 4-bit, so prefer FP8 or QAT for them — and QAT with teacher-probability targets is itself a form of distillation (Gemma 3 QAT did exactly this).

A large study from late twenty twenty-four, over half a million evaluations on the Llama three point one family, found F P eight weight-and-activation essentially lossless, well-tuned integer eight-bit weight-and-activation within about one to three percent, and four-bit weight-only surprisingly competitive with eight-bit integer. Finally, quantization-aware training, Q A T. Instead of printing after the fact, you let the model practise with the reduced inks during a short fine-tuning phase: the forward pass simulates the rounding, and the weights learn to sit in places that survive it. It recovers much of the quality lost at four-bit, at the price of a training run. Google shipped quantization-aware-trained four-bit Gemma checkpoints that way. And notice a detail from their report, because it's going to matter in a minute: the fine-tuning targets were the probabilities from the unquantized model. The reduced-ink print was trained to match the master's full palette of confidences. That's distillation, inside quantization. [PAUSE 1s] So, the print shop's advantages. It's fast: post-training quantization is an afternoon, not a project. It's cheap: no teacher data generation, no training run for P T Q. It keeps the architecture, so every tool, every prompt, every tokenizer behavior is unchanged. It's reversible: you still have the master painting. And it's widely supported: serving engines load F P eight, A W Q and G P T Q checkpoints directly, and vendors now ship open-weights models with four-bit expert weights so a hundred-billion-plus-parameter model fits on one eighty-gigabyte G P U. And the disadvantages. The quality loss is not uniform, and that's the dangerous part. Average benchmark scores barely move, while specific capabilities fall off a cliff. A study from twenty twenty-five on long-context tasks found eight-bit methods lost under one percent on average, but four-bit methods lost substantially on long inputs, up to fifty-nine percent on some tasks, getting worse as context length grew and worse again for non-English inputs. Math, code, strict formatting and tool-call argument generation are the other usual casualties. Calibration data matters: calibrate on generic web text, deploy on financial documents full of numbers, and the outlier statistics don't match. And the speedup is hardware-dependent; a four-bit checkpoint on the wrong kernel can be slower than eight-bit. [PAUSE 2s] ## Tradeoffs, pitfalls, and a war story Stand back and look at the whole wall again. Distillation, on the left, changes what the model is. Quantization, on the right, changes how precisely the same model is stored. Here's the war story, the shape of which comes up again and again. A team serving an internal assistant for operations staff quantized their model to four-bit weight-only to fit it on a cheaper G P U. They ran their standard evaluation: a general knowledge benchmark and a couple of hundred short question-and-answer pairs. Scores within a point of the original. Shipped. Within a week, the downstream agent that called tools started failing intermittently. The model was still choosing the right tool, but the JSON arguments had subtle problems: a date format drifting, an account identifier with a digit transposed, a closing brace missing on long payloads. And a separate long-document summarization feature had started repeating itself on inputs past about thirty thousand tokens. The fix was to go back to eight-bit floating point for that workload, keep four-bit for a short-context classification endpoint where it was genuinely harmless, and add the missing evaluation slices, tool-argument exactness and long-context faithfulness, to the release gate. The lesson you want to say out loud: evaluate quantized models on your task's hardest slices, not on averages. The distillation-side version: a student distilled on six months of traffic matched the teacher on an evaluation set drawn from those same months, then fell apart when a new product shifted the question mix. It had learned the distribution, not the skill. Keep the teacher as a routed fallback, and plan re-distillation as traffic shifts. Now, when do you combine them? Often, and in a particular order: distill first, then quantize the student. The apprentice paints the small canvas; then the print shop reproduces the small canvas with fewer inks. Distilling first gets you the big architectural win; quantizing second gets you another two to four times on memory. Two cautions. Small models have less redundancy, so they're more fragile under aggressive quantization than big ones. And if you plan to quantize, consider making the student's training quantization-aware, which, as we saw, is itself a form of distillation. A quick decision framework you can say out loud. Need a fast win this week on hardware that supports it: F P eight first, nearly free quality-wise, half the memory. Memory-bound, small batches, short context: four-bit weight-only with A W Q or G P T Q, and test the hard slices. Need ten times cheaper on a narrow, high-volume, stable task, with a few weeks to spare: distill into a small model, then quantize it. If the task is broad, open-ended, or changes monthly: be very careful with distillation, lean on quantization plus smarter routing. And whatever you pick, in a regulated environment, treat each variant as a new model version: same evaluation gate, logged lineage back to the teacher and the calibration data, and the ability to roll back to the master painting. [PAUSE 2s] ## The interview angle When they ask "what are the pros and cons of distillation versus quantization," a strong answer, spoken in about ninety seconds, sounds like this. "They attack cost from different directions. Distillation trains a smaller student model to imitate a larger teacher, so it changes the architecture: fewer parameters, less compute, less memory and a smaller K V cache per token. Three flavors: logit-level, matching the teacher's full distribution, which needs its logits; sequence-level, fine-tuning on the teacher's outputs, which works even with a hosted teacher; and on-policy, where the student generates and the teacher grades each token, which fixes exposure bias. Pros: order-of-magnitude savings and specialization. Cons: a real training project, a capability ceiling, off-distribution degradation, and a new model to validate, with licensing questions. Quantization keeps the same model and stores its numbers in fewer bits. Post-training, it's hours of work: F P eight is close to lossless on modern G P Us, and four-bit weight-only with A W Q or G P T Q quarters weight memory, which helps memory-bound decoding. Q A T recovers quality with a short training phase. The cons are that the damage is non-uniform: averages look fine while long-context, math, and structured outputs like tool-call arguments degrade, and the speed-up depends on hardware and kernels. In practice I'd start with F P eight, go to four-bit only with slice-level evaluation on the hard cases, and reserve distillation for narrow, high-volume, stable tasks, then quantize the student too. Every variant goes through the same evaluation gate as a new model version." [PAUSE 1s] Now the follow-ups, and the traps. Follow-up one: "Does quantization reduce latency or throughput?" Trap: saying "both, always." Better: weight-only quantization mostly helps the memory-bound decode phase, so time per output token improves at small batch sizes; weight-plus-activation formats help compute-bound, large-batch throughput. And quantizing the K V cache is a separate knob that lets you fit more concurrent sequences. Follow-up two: "Why not just use a smaller off-the-shelf model instead of distilling?" Sometimes you should; try it first. Distillation earns its cost when you have lots of task traffic and the teacher is clearly better on it. Follow-up three: "How would you know the quantized model is good enough?" Trap: "perplexity stayed within a point." Better: perplexity is a sanity check, not an acceptance test. Run the task evaluation set with slices for long inputs, numbers, tool-call argument exactness and refusal behavior, compare against the unquantized model with paired statistics, and shadow production traffic before switching. [PAUSE 2s] ## Sixty-second recap Back to the gallery wall one last time. In the center, the master painting: the big teacher model, every stroke a parameter, painted with tens of thousands of shades. To the left, the apprentice's small canvas: distillation. A different, smaller model that learns by seeing the master's full hesitation at every stroke, or by copying finished paintings, or best of all by painting on its own while the master grades each stroke. Smaller network, training project, capability ceiling. To the right, the print shop: quantization. Same painting, same size, fewer inks. Smaller patches with their own ink sets, protecting the important strokes with A W Q, nudging neighbors to absorb rounding error with G P T Q, and practising with the reduced inks through Q A T. A quick, cheap, reversible win, with damage that hides in long contexts, numbers and structured outputs. The strongest move is often both: paint the small canvas, then print it with fewer inks, and inspect the result on your hardest slices before it ever reaches a customer.
