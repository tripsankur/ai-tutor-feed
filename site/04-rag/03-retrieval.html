Retrieval: BM25, Hybrid, Reranking, Query Rewriting

17 min listen · The retrieval funnel, top to bottom: rewrite the question, send a keyword scout and a meaning scout in parallel, fuse their lists with reciprocal rank fusion, and let a cross-encoder judge pick the final few. How B M twenty-five works, why hybrid wins, and what each stage costs in latency.

Facts verified: 2026-09-25

One-sentence thesis

Production retrieval is a funnel: rewrite the question, run lexical (BM25) and dense retrieval in parallel because their blind spots are opposite, fuse the lists with reciprocal rank fusion, then let a cross-encoder reranker pick the final few. Each stage trades latency for precision.

The mental image: the retrieval funnel (top to bottom)

  1. Rewriting desk at the wide mouth: condense, expand, decompose, filter.
  2. Two side-by-side channels: keyword scout (left, inverted index + BM25) and meaning scout (right, vector search on the map of meaning).
  3. Merge table: reciprocal rank fusion.
  4. Judge's bench: cross-encoder reranker, reads query + candidate together.
  5. Spout: top 5–20 chunks to the LLM.

Millions at the top → ~50 per scout → one fused list → a handful out the bottom.

flowchart TB

    RW --> BM["Keyword scout: BM25 on inverted index (top 50)"]
    RW --> VS["Meaning scout: ANN vector search (top 50)"]

BM25

score(D, Q) = Σ over query terms t of IDF(t) · tf(t,D)·(k1+1) / (tf(t,D) + k1·(1 − b + b·|D|/avgdl))

Component Intuition Default
IDF Rare terms (error codes, names) weigh more —
tf saturation (k1) Repeats help with diminishing returns k1 = 1.2 (Lucene)
Length normalisation (b) Long docs don't win by length b = 0.75

Strength: exact terms, rare identifiers, explainable. Weakness: vocabulary mismatch ("end recurring payment" vs "cancel subscription").

Reciprocal Rank Fusion

RRF(d) = Σ over lists 1 / (k + rank_list(d)), k = 60 (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, SIGIR 2009). Default hybrid fusion in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Azure AI Search, Weaviate and others. Uses ranks only, so no score-scale normalisation is needed.

def rrf(result_lists, k=60, weights=None):
    weights = weights or [1.0] * len(result_lists)
    scores = {}
    for w, results in zip(weights, result_lists):
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(results, start=1):
            scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0.0) + w / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

fused = rrf([bm25_ids, vector_ids])[:50]

Latency budget (typical)

Rewrite ~100s of ms (small model) + parallel BM25/vector ~10s of ms + RRF ≈ 0 + rerank 50 candidates ~100–300 ms ⇒ often 0.5–1 s before generation starts.

5 interview questions

Q1. Why not just vector search? Dense retrieval captures paraphrase but misses exact identifiers, rare names and codes; BM25 is the reverse. Hybrid + RRF covers both for tens of ms of extra latency, which is why it's the default in mature engines.

Q2. Explain RRF and why k = 60. Score = Σ 1/(k + rank) across lists; uses ranks, not incomparable raw scores. k = 60 (from the 2009 paper) flattens the curve so agreement across lists beats a single list's top rank. Weight lists if eval data supports it.

Q3. Where does a reranker fit and what does it cost? After first-stage retrieval/fusion, on the top ~50–150. Cross-encoder reads query+doc jointly: far more accurate, no precomputation, one pass per candidate (~100s of ms). Usually the biggest quality lift without re-indexing.

Q4. How does BM25 work? Sum over query terms of IDF × saturating term frequency (k1 ≈ 1.2), normalised by document length relative to average (b ≈ 0.75).

Q5. When do you rewrite queries? Always condense conversational follow-ups; use multi-query/decomposition where eval shows recall gaps; avoid paraphrasing exact-match queries; budget the extra LLM call and the multiplied searches.

[PAUSE 2s] ## Under the hood: the keyword scout, B M twenty-five Let's start in the left channel, with the keyword scout, because this is the part people who came to R A G through language models often never learned. The keyword scout's index book is called an inverted index. At indexing time, every card is broken into terms, lowercased, sometimes reduced to its stem, so that "refreshing" and "refreshed" both become "refresh." Then for every term, the index stores the list of cards that contain it and how many times. At query time, the scout looks up each word of the question, pulls those lists, and scores every card that appears in any of them. Looking up a word is nearly instant, even across tens of millions of cards, which is why this technology has powered search engines for decades. The scoring formula is B M twenty-five, short for "best matching," the twenty-fifth variant of a family of formulas from the nineteen nineties. It's still the default ranking in Lucene, and so in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, and it rewards three intuitions. One: rare words matter more. If the question contains "the" and "X K seven two nine," a card matching "the" tells you nothing, because nearly every card contains it, while a card matching "X K seven two nine" tells you a lot. This is inverse document frequency: the fewer cards a term appears in, the more it's worth. It's exactly why the keyword scout nails error codes, product names, and people's names: they're rare, so they carry enormous weight. Two: repetition helps, but with diminishing returns. A card that mentions "refund" three times is probably more about refunds than a card that mentions it once. But a card that mentions it forty times isn't forty times more relevant; it's probably spam or boilerplate. B M twenty-five saturates: each extra occurrence adds less than the one before. A parameter called k one controls how fast it saturates, and the common default is one point two. Three: long documents shouldn't win just by being long. A thousand-token card will contain more words by accident than a hundred-token card. B M twenty-five normalizes for length against the average card length, controlled by a parameter called b, commonly set to zero point seven five, meaning mostly, but not fully, normalized. So the keyword scout is fast, cheap, explainable, you can see exactly which words matched, and superb at exact terms. Its blind spot is the mirror image of the meaning scout's: it has no idea that "end my recurring payment" and "cancel a subscription" mean the same thing. Zero shared words, zero score. That's called the vocabulary mismatch problem, and it's what embeddings were built to solve. In the right channel, the meaning scout does what we covered in episode one: embed the question, find the nearest arrows on the map. Great at paraphrase, weak at exact codes. Two scouts, with opposite blind spots. That's the whole argument for running both. [PAUSE 1s] ## The merge table: hybrid search and reciprocal rank fusion Now both scouts arrive at the merge table, each holding a ranked list of fifty. How do you combine them? The naive idea is to add their scores. But their scores are on completely different scales. B M twenty-five scores might run from zero to thirty, depending on the query. Cosine similarities might all sit between zero point six and zero point nine. Adding them is like adding temperatures in Celsius to prices in yen. You can normalize each list to a zero-to-one range first and take a weighted sum, and some systems do exactly that, but the result is sensitive to outliers and to how scores happen to be distributed for each query. The elegant answer, and the default in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Azure A I Search, Weaviate, and several others, is reciprocal rank fusion. It throws the scores away and uses only the positions. For each card, look at its rank in each list. Its fused score is one divided by sixty plus its rank in the keyword list, plus one divided by sixty plus its rank in the vector list. A card ranked first in both lists gets the highest possible fused score. A card ranked first by one scout and absent from the other still scores well. A card ranked tenth by both beats a card ranked fifth by one and missing from the other. Why sixty? It's a smoothing constant from the original two thousand nine paper by Cormack, Clarke, and Büttcher, and it's held up remarkably well. It stops the very top rank from dominating: without it, being ranked first would be worth twice as much as being ranked second, which is too steep. With sixty, the drop-off is gentle, so agreement between scouts matters more than any single scout's enthusiasm. Some systems let you weight one list more than the other; that's a reasonable dial to tune once you have evaluation data. Walk our cold-open question through it. The meaning scout on the right ranks it maybe twelfth, among general token-refresh articles. At the merge table, the article scores well from both, and rises to the top of the fused list. The user gets the right answer, and nothing in the vector index had to change.
