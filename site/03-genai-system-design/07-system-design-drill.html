9 min listen · Fourteen rapid-fire interview questions across the whole GenAI system design track — the production corridor, designing a RAG system, latency and cost, evals, guardrails and prompt injection, and scaling and multi-tenancy. Answer aloud in the pause, then compare.

Facts verified: 2026-09-25

One-sentence thesis

A GenAI system design answer is the same request corridor every time — gateway, guards, orchestrator, context, routed models, tracing, eval gate — and each question in an interview is really asking you to zoom into one room of it with numbers.

Why hybrid search plus a reranker, rather than vectors alone? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: vectors catch paraphrase but miss exact identifiers like product codes; keyword search catches identifiers but misses paraphrase. Reciprocal rank fusion merges the two ranked lists without normalizing scores. Then a cross-encoder reranker re-scores the top fifty or so and keeps the best five — far more accurate than similarity, and cheap at that size. [PAUSE 2s] Question five. High time to first token means queueing, long prompts, prompt-cache misses, or retrieval and tool calls running in series. Long generation means too many output tokens — cap them, ask for concision, lower reasoning effort, or route to a faster model. And stream, so the first words appear in about a second. [PAUSE 2s] Question six. Costs grew five times while traffic only doubled. What's the likely cause, and the fix? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: usually longer prompts or a collapsed prompt-cache hit rate — for example, someone put a timestamp at the top of the prompt, so the cached prefix never matches. Order prompts stable-first, variable-last, alert on cache hit rate, then trim tokens, move non-urgent work to batch interfaces at roughly half price, and route easy traffic to smaller models. [PAUSE 2s] Question seven. How do you build a golden set, and what's the most common mistake? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: start with fifty to a hundred expert-labelled cases drawn from real production traffic — especially thumbs-downs and escalations — slice them by intent and difficulty, keep a held-out portion, and version the set. The common mistake is watching only the average: one slice can collapse by twenty points while the overall score barely moves, so gate per slice. [PAUSE 2s] Question eight. Name the biases of L L M as a judge and how to counter each. [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: position bias — run pairwise comparisons in both orders and only count consistent wins. Verbosity bias — put conciseness in the rubric and track answer length. Self-preference — use a judge from a different model family. And vague scales — use binary or three-point rubrics, one criterion per judge, reasoning before the verdict. Most important: measure judge-human agreement on a labelled sample before you trust it. [PAUSE 2s] Question nine. Your new prompt scores eighty-two percent against eighty on two hundred cases. Do you ship? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: that difference is noise — the ninety-five percent confidence interval on two hundred cases at eighty percent is roughly plus or minus five and a half points. Do a paired comparison of which cases flipped in each direction, confirm no critical slice regressed, and check latency and cost. Ship it only for a real reason, such as equal quality at lower cost. [PAUSE 2s] Question ten. How do you defend against prompt injection? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: assume it can't be fully prevented, because the model can't reliably separate instructions from data. Layer classifiers on user input and on retrieved content, delimit untrusted text, and validate output — including never auto-rendering links or images to arbitrary domains. The real control is blast radius: least-privilege tools running as the user, human approval for irreversible actions, and breaking the lethal trifecta. [PAUSE 2s] Question eleven.
