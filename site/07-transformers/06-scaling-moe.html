Scaling Laws, Mixture-of-Experts, Long Context

16 min listen · How the field decides how big to build a model and how much to feed it, why nearly every large open model is now a mixture-of-experts that only wakes a small slice of itself per token, and what a million-token context window really costs and really delivers.

Facts verified: 2026-09-25

One-sentence thesis

Scaling laws tell you how to spend training compute (and why production models are deliberately overtrained), mixture-of-experts decouples what a model knows (total parameters) from what each token costs (active parameters), and long context is mostly a memory-and-attention bill whose effective length is shorter than the advertised one.

The mental image: the library (seen from above)

  • Chart on the south wall above the entrance = scaling law; three dials: staff (parameters N), books read (tokens D), hours of work (compute C). A fourth dial now: thinking time (test-time compute).
  • Receptionist at the front left = MoE router; specialist desks along the north wall = experts. Each visitor (token) goes to only top-k desks.
  • Long reading table down the centre = context window; the reader skims the middle ("lost in the middle").

Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020): loss falls as a power law in N, D, C over many orders of magnitude. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022) fit:

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, \qquad C \approx 6ND$$

Compute-optimal: N and D scale ~equally with C → ≈ 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat Gopher (280B) at the same compute.

Regime Tokens/param Example Why
Compute-optimal (Chinchilla) ~20 Chinchilla 70B / 1.4T minimizes training cost for a target loss
Inference-aware (overtrained) 100s–1000s Llama 3 8B / 15T (~1,875) smaller model = cheaper forever at serving
Test-time scaling n/a reasoning models spend more tokens thinking per query

Rule of thumb for training FLOPs: C ≈ 6 × N_active × D. Scaling predicts loss, not specific capabilities.

Mixture-of-Experts

flowchart LR
    T["Token hidden state"] --> A["Self-attention (shared by all tokens)"]
    A --> R{"Router: scores per expert, pick top-k"}
    R -->|w1| E1["Expert 3 (FFN)"]
    R -->|w2| E2["Expert 41 (FFN)"]
    R -.->|idle| E3["Other experts (resident in memory, not computed)"]
    SH["Shared expert (always on)"] --> SUM
    E1 --> SUM["Weighted sum"]
    E2 --> SUM
    SUM --> O["Next layer"]

$$y = \sum_{i \in \text{TopK}(g(x))} g_i(x)\, E_i(x), \qquad g(x) = \text{softmax}(W_r x)$$

Costs and pitfalls: - Memory ≈ total params (all experts resident) even though compute ≈ active params. - Expert parallelism → all-to-all token shuffles across GPUs; interconnect becomes the bottleneck. - Load balancing / routing collapse → auxiliary balance loss, capacity factor, token dropping; DeepSeek-V3 uses a per-expert bias adjustment (auxiliary-loss-free). - Batch effect: at large batch nearly all experts are active every step → benchmark at real concurrency. - Fine-tuning can destabilize routers.

# Minimal top-k MoE layer (PyTorch-style sketch)
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
class MoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_experts=8, k=2, d_ff=4096):
        super().__init__()
        self.router = nn.Linear(d, n_experts, bias=False)
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList(nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, d_ff), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d_ff, d)) for _ in range(n_experts))
        self.k = k
    def forward(self, x):                      # x: [tokens, d]
        w, idx = self.router(x).topk(self.k, dim=-1)
        w = F.softmax(w, dim=-1)
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for slot in range(self.k):
            for e in idx[:, slot].unique():
                m = idx[:, slot] == e
                out[m] += w[m, slot, None] * self.experts[e](x[m])
        return out

Long context

Three obstacles: 1. Attention cost O(n²) in prefill → FlashAttention (exact, tiled, O(n) memory), ring/context parallelism across GPUs, hybrid stacks mixing full attention with sliding-window/linear layers. 2. KV cache grows linearly with n (see episode 5) → at 1M tokens, tens–hundreds of GB per request. 3. Positions beyond training length → RoPE scaling: position interpolation, NTK-aware, YaRN (frequency-dependent scaling + attention temperature; 128K extension with <0.1% of pre-training data in fine-tuning).

Quality: Lost in the Middle (Liu et al. 2023) — U-shaped accuracy vs position of relevant info. RULER (NVIDIA) separates claimed vs effective context length using multi-needle, multi-hop, aggregation tasks. Many frontier models advertise ≥1M tokens in 2026; effective length is shorter and task-dependent.

Practical: long context for one big artefact needing holistic reasoning; RAG for large/changing corpora; instructions first, question last; prefix-cache shared documents; evaluate at your real length.

5 interview questions

Q1. What is the Chinchilla result and why do labs violate it? For fixed training compute, parameters and tokens should grow together (~20 tokens/param); prior large models were under-trained. Labs overtrain smaller models (e.g., 8B on 15T tokens) because inference cost over the model's lifetime dominates, and quality keeps improving log-linearly.

Q2. Explain total vs active parameters in MoE and the serving implications. Total parameters (all experts) determine memory footprint and knowledge capacity; active parameters (top-k experts + shared parts) determine FLOPs per token. Serving needs memory for all weights, expert parallelism, all-to-all communication, and good load balance; large batches activate most experts.

Q3. What is routing collapse and how is it prevented? The router over-uses a few experts, which get better and attract more traffic while others starve. Mitigations: auxiliary load-balancing loss, capacity factors and token dropping, noise in routing, or DeepSeek-V3-style bias adjustment without an auxiliary loss.

Q4. How do models extend context beyond training length? RoPE-based models rescale rotary frequencies (position interpolation, NTK-aware, YaRN) so new positions map into seen angle ranges, then fine-tune briefly on long sequences; systems side uses FlashAttention, context/ring parallelism, KV compression.

Q5. When would you choose long context over RAG? For one bounded artefact requiring holistic reasoning (codebase, contract). Prefer RAG for large, changing corpora or cost/latency-sensitive paths. Effective context is shorter than advertised and mid-context recall degrades, so evaluate at your real length and combine both when needed.
