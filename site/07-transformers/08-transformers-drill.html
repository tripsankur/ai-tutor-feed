Welcome back to the AI Tutor. This is the Track Seven drill — fifteen questions across the whole transformer track, from recurrent networks to reasoning-model training. Same format as always: I ask, you answer out loud in the pause, then I give you a strong answer to compare against. The questions run roughly in the order of the track, so you will hear exactly where your gaps are. Ready. [PAUSE 2s] Question one. Why did transformers replace recurrent neural networks? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: recurrent networks walk the sequence one step at a time through a fixed-size hidden state, so training cannot be parallelised along the sequence, and information between distant tokens has to survive a long chain, which causes forgetting and vanishing gradients. The transformer removed recurrence entirely: every token attends directly to every other token, giving a constant path length and turning the whole sequence into large parallel matrix multiplications that G P Us are built for. The price is compute and memory that grow with the square of the sequence length, and position has to be injected explicitly. [PAUSE 2s] Question two. Explain self-attention in plain words, then technically. [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: each token asks a question, advertises a label, and carries content; it compares its question against every other token's label, turns the scores into percentages, and takes that share of each token's content, coming out context-aware. Technically: learned query, key and value projections, scaled dot-product scores, a softmax, and a weighted sum of the values. [PAUSE 2s] Question three. Why do we divide the attention scores by the square root of the head dimension? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: dot products grow in magnitude with dimension, and large scores push the softmax toward a near one-hot distribution, where gradients vanish. Scaling keeps the distribution soft, so the model stays trainable. [PAUSE 2s] Question four. What does FlashAttention change, and what does it not change? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: it computes exact attention in tiles held in fast on-chip memory, with an online softmax, so it never materialises the full score matrix — memory becomes linear in sequence length and wall-clock time drops a lot. It does not change the result, and the compute is still quadratic. [PAUSE 2s] Question five. Why multiple attention heads, and what is grouped-query attention for? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: each head is a smaller attention with its own projections, so a layer can track several relationships at once — coreference, syntax, adjacency — for roughly the cost of one full-width head. Grouped-query attention lets groups of query heads share key and value heads, which shrinks the K V cache by the group factor — thirty-two query heads over eight key-value heads is four times smaller — with only a small quality cost. [PAUSE 2s] Question six. How does rotary position embedding encode position, and why does it matter for long context? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: RoPE rotates query and key vectors by angles proportional to position, at many frequencies, so their dot product depends only on relative distance. Because position lives in those frequencies, context extension methods like position interpolation and YaRN work by rescaling them, then fine-tuning briefly on long documents. [PAUSE 2s] Question seven. Pre-norm or post-norm, and what is the residual stream doing? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: the residual stream is the running sum each sub-layer reads from and adds back to, which keeps a clean identity path for gradients through a deep stack. Modern models use pre-norm — normalising inside each branch, usually with R M S norm — because it trains stably at depth, whereas the original post-norm design becomes unstable and needs careful warm-up. [PAUSE 2s] Question eight. What is the training objective of a decoder-only L L M, and why does it scale so well? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss. The causal mask lets every position be trained in parallel with teacher forcing, so one sequence of N tokens yields N graded examples, and raw text is its own label, so it scales to trillions of tokens without human labelling. Instruction-following comes later, in post-training. [PAUSE 2s] Question nine. Why can a model struggle to count the letters in "strawberry," and what is the business consequence of tokenization? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: byte-pair encoding cuts the word into a few multi-letter pieces, so the model never sees individual letters and has to have memorised each piece's spelling. The business consequence is that token counts depend on the tokenizer and the language — some languages need several times more tokens for the same meaning — so cost and context budgets must be measured on real traffic in every target language. [PAUSE 2s] Question ten. Why is decode memory-bound, and what does the K V cache cost? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: each decode step produces one token per sequence but must stream all the model's weights plus that sequence's cached keys and values from memory, so the G P U waits on bandwidth, not arithmetic; batching amortises the weight reads. The cache is about two, times layers, times key-value heads, times head size, times bytes, per token — roughly a hundred and twenty-eight kilobytes per token for an eight-billion model with grouped-query attention, or sixteen gigabytes at a hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens. PagedAttention, prefix caching and cache quantization are the standard mitigations. [PAUSE 2s] Question eleven. How does speculative decoding work, and does it change the output? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: a cheap draft — a small model, extra heads, or an EAGLE-style draft head — proposes several tokens, and the target model verifies them all in one forward pass, keeping the longest accepted prefix and substituting its own token at the first rejection. With the rejection-sampling acceptance rule the output distribution is identical to the target model alone, so it is lossless; the speedup depends on the acceptance rate and shrinks at large batch sizes. [PAUSE 2s] Question twelve. What did Chinchilla show, and why do production models ignore it? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: for a fixed training compute budget, parameters and training tokens should grow roughly equally — about twenty tokens per parameter — and earlier large models were under-trained. Production models are deliberately overtrained far past that point, like an eight-billion model on fifteen trillion tokens, because a smaller model is cheaper to serve for its whole lifetime. [PAUSE 2s] Question thirteen. A mixture-of-experts model has six hundred and seventy-one billion parameters but thirty-seven billion active. What does that mean for serving? [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: a router sends each token to a few experts, so compute per token is like a thirty-seven-billion dense model, but every expert must be resident, so memory is like a six-hundred-and-seventy-one-billion model. You need expert parallelism across many G P Us, fast interconnect for the all-to-all traffic, and good load balancing — and you must benchmark at real batch sizes, where most experts end up active every step. [PAUSE 2s] Question fourteen. Explain LoRA and QLoRA, and when you would fine-tune at all. [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: LoRA freezes the pre-trained weights and learns a low-rank update — two thin matrices whose product is added to selected layers — so trainable parameters and optimiser memory drop by orders of magnitude and adapters can be merged or hot-swapped. QLoRA keeps the frozen base in four-bit, so even a sixty-five-billion model fits on one forty-eight-gigabyte G P U. Fine-tune for behaviour — format, tone, tool use, distillation into a smaller model — and use retrieval for knowledge. [PAUSE 2s] Question fifteen. Compare R L H F, D P O, and G R P O. [PAUSE 6s] Strong answer: R L H F trains a reward model on human preference pairs and optimises the policy online with P P O under a K L tether — powerful but heavy, with four models in memory and a risk of reward hacking. D P O turns the same preference pairs into a direct loss against a frozen reference — cheap and stable, but offline. G R P O powers reasoning models: sample a group of attempts per problem, score them with a verifiable reward like unit tests or an exact answer, and push up the above-average attempts with no value model — refined by variants like DAPO, and increasingly combined with on-policy distillation from a stronger teacher.
